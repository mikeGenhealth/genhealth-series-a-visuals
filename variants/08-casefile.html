Every claim begins as a story. Someone needs care. A doctor recommends a course. Forms are filled, codes are looked up, policies consulted, calls are made, faxes wait in queues. Most of the time, the human in question never sees this work — only the bill at the end. This is the work GenHealth does. What follows is a single, ordinary case — Jane Doe, prescribed a CPAP — and a record of the four chapters in which our AI employees moved her care from referral to paid.
Chapter I

The fax arrives.

P. 12 / 96

At 09:14 on a Tuesday, four pages arrive on the supplier's fax line. They are scanned at 200 dpi, slightly skewed, and signed in pen. A Certificate of Medical Necessity for one Jane Doe.

Inside the old workflow, the fax would sit. A queue of forty-six other faxes. A clinician to sort them, a coordinator to key them, a chance — small but never zero — that a field would be misread or a digit transposed.

Inside ours, the document is read in seconds. Forty-six discrete fields are extracted and mapped: patient identifiers, ordering physician NPI, diagnosis, equipment HCPCS, sleep-study date, AHI value. A patient record is matched in Brightree. The intake queue advances by one.

No one keys it. No one misses it. The next fax is already being read.

A claim is the policy meeting the patient. The agent loads them both.

It pulls Aetna's CPAP coverage policy in its current revision. It reads Jane's record — the sleep study, the Epworth score, the face-to-face note from Dr. Patel, the signed written order. It compares each criterion to the evidence. It cites the line of chart that satisfies it.

The decision is not produced from a guess. It is a defensible record: four criteria, met by four explicit citations. Anyone — clinician, payer, auditor — can open the file and see what the agent saw, why it concluded as it did, and where in the chart the evidence lives.

This is the part where, traditionally, hours go by. For Jane, it took 42 seconds.

A medical decision is also a citation.
Chapter III

The phone call.

P. 54 / 96

Some data only voice can collect.

Jane's mailing address has changed. Her secondary insurance is new. There is a question only she can answer about delivery preferences — to her home, or to her son's, where she stays during the week.

The agent dials at 14:02. It identifies itself, plainly. It asks the questions it needs and only those. It captures the consent. It writes the answers back into the same intake record where the fax began. Nothing is lost between systems because everything happens in one.

The call lasts three minutes, forty-two seconds. Jane returns to her afternoon.

From referral to paid: twenty-one days. No reworks. No appeals. No queue of paper waiting on a person who could only ever do this one case at a time.
